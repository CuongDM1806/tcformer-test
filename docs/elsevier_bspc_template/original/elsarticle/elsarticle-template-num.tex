\begin{abstract}
Cross-subject motor-imagery electroencephalography (MI-EEG) decoding is hindered by low signal-to-noise ratios, scarce labeled data, and strong distribution shifts between subjects and sessions. This study proposes SSM-DAN, a domain-adaptive selective state-space network that couples a multi-scale convolutional front-end with bidirectional selective state-space blocks to capture both local and long-range temporal patterns, and reduces subject shift through hierarchical alignment, including a learned source--target normalization layer and label-free test-time adaptation. The framework was evaluated on three public MI-EEG datasets using leave-one-subject-out validation with chronologically separated training, adaptation, and test sessions. On BCI Competition IV-2a, it reached a mean accuracy of 76.12\%, significantly higher than its source-only variant (64.35\%; $p=0.0039$) and its variant without test-time adaptation (72.15\%; $p=0.0039$). It also achieved 84.09\% on BCI Competition IV-2b and 84.33\% on Zhou2016, where it outperformed a matched TCFormer baseline on all four target subjects. Ablation studies showed that the contribution of each adaptation component depends on the dataset. These results indicate that combining selective state-space modeling with learned, hierarchical alignment improves cross-subject MI decoding without target labels.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Motor-imagery brain--computer interfaces (MI-BCIs) decode movement intentions from non-invasive electroencephalography (EEG) for rehabilitation, communication, and control. Their event-related desynchronization and synchronization signatures are weak, non-stationary, and spatially distributed \cite{pfurtscheller1999erd}, and they vary between subjects and sessions, so a model trained on existing users often degrades on a new user, while calibrating every user with labeled trials is time-consuming. Cross-subject decoding must therefore learn discriminative spatial--temporal structure and handle inter-subject shift without target labels.

Common spatial pattern (CSP) and filter-bank CSP rely on hand-designed, subject-specific stages that transfer poorly \cite{ramoser2000optimal,ang2012filterbank}. ConvNets and EEGNet learn filters end to end \cite{schirrmeister2017deep,lawhern2018eegnet} but capture long-range context only through depth or dilation, while hybrid decoders such as EEG Conformer, ATCNet, and TCFormer add self-attention and dilated temporal convolutional networks (TCNs) \cite{song2023eegconformer,altaheri2023atcnet,altaheri2025tcformer} at quadratic cost \cite{vaswani2017attention} and with high data demand. Selective state-space models offer linear-complexity sequence modeling \cite{gu2022s4,gu2024mamba}, and recent MI decoders combine convolution with Mamba \cite{guo2025mimamba,yang2025stmamba}, but they focus on architecture and do not address subject shift.

Domain generalization learns subject-invariant features from source subjects only \cite{liu2023stdg,zhong2025eegdg,zheng2025crosssubject}, whereas unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) also uses unlabeled target EEG through Riemannian or Euclidean alignment \cite{zanini2018riemannian,he2020euclidean}, MK-MMD \cite{long2015dan}, adversarial learning \cite{ganin2016dann,tang2020cdan}, or class prototypes \cite{zhang2024prototypeda}; AutoDIAL aligns domains inside normalization layers by learning how to mix source and target statistics \cite{carlucci2017autodial}. Test-time adaptation further reduces residual shift at inference through information maximization \cite{liang2020shot} or normalization-only updates \cite{wang2021tent}. However, fixed-strength alignment risks negative transfer when the shift is small, pooled-feature alignment can overlook temporal mismatch, and test-time adaptation uses evaluation-session signals, which must be disclosed and kept separate from labels.

To our knowledge, selective state-space MI decoders have not been coupled with target adaptation, and existing EEG adaptation methods neither learn how strongly to align nor align temporal representations. We therefore propose SSM-DAN, which couples a bidirectional selective state-space encoder with hierarchical, label-free adaptation and is evaluated under a chronological leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) protocol that keeps target labels hidden throughout. The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item an end-to-end decoder that combines multi-scale convolution, alternating forward/backward selective state-space blocks, and local--global TCN fusion;
    \item a hierarchical adaptation strategy that combines Riemannian alignment, an AutoDIAL normalization layer, adversarial and MK-MMD alignment, and constrained test-time adaptation within a chronological, leakage-controlled LOSO protocol; and
    \item an evaluation on three public MI-EEG datasets with subject-wise accuracy, Cohen's $\kappa$, statistical tests, efficiency analysis, and component ablations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\begin{figure*}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/architecture_overview.pdf}
    \caption{Overview of SSM-DAN: training with labeled source and unlabeled target EEG (Stage~1), label-free test-time adaptation on the unlabeled evaluation sessions $\mathcal{T}_e$ (Stage~2), and prediction (Stage~3).}
    \label{fig:architecture_overview}
\end{figure*}

SSM-DAN has two parts (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_overview}). An EEG encoder combines multi-scale convolutions for local spectral--spatial features, bidirectional selective state-space blocks for long-range dynamics, and a temporal convolutional fusion head (Sections~\ref{subsec:conv_frontend}--\ref{subsec:tcn}). A hierarchical adaptation scheme reduces subject shift at three levels: Riemannian whitening of the input, AutoDIAL normalization with adversarial and discrepancy losses on the features, and test-time adaptation of the predictions (Sections~\ref{subsec:ra}--\ref{subsec:imtta}). The model is trained on labeled source-subject trials and unlabeled target-subject trials (Stage~1), adapted to the unlabeled evaluation trials (Stage~2), and then used for prediction (Stage~3).

\subsection{Multi-scale convolutional encoder}
\label{subsec:conv_frontend}

\begin{figure*}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/encoder_detail.pdf}
    \caption{Multi-scale temporal--spatial front-end. (a) Three temporal branches, depthwise electrode filtering, grouped refinement, and channel-group attention produce $H_c$. (b) Channel-group attention: grouped $1\times1$ squeeze--excitation yields one sigmoid weight per temporal scale, which rescales that scale's 16 channels and is added residually.}
    \label{fig:encoder_detail}
\end{figure*}

Each trial $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times T}$ ($C$ electrodes, $T$ samples) passes through three temporal convolutions with kernels of 20, 32, and 64 samples, as in TCFormer \cite{altaheri2025tcformer}, followed by depthwise spatial filtering, pooling, and grouped refinement to 16 channels per scale (Fig.~\ref{fig:encoder_detail}). A residual channel-group attention reweights the three scales, and a second pooling yields the local feature sequence $H_c\in\mathbb{R}^{48\times L}$.

\newpage
\subsection{Bidirectional selective state-space encoder}
\label{subsec:ssm}

\begin{figure*}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ssm_block.pdf}
    \caption{Selective SSM block. Backward blocks reverse and restore the temporal axis around the mixer; forward blocks omit these operations.}
    \label{fig:ssm_block}
\end{figure*}

To capture trial-wide dependencies, $H_c$ passes through five selective state-space blocks \cite{gu2022s4,gu2024mamba}, each driving a diagonal recurrence with its projected input $u_t$:
\begin{align}
    h_t &= \exp(\Delta_t A)\odot h_{t-1} + \Delta_t B_t\odot u_t, \\
    z_t &= C_t^\top h_t + d\odot u_t ,
\end{align}
where $A=-\exp(A_{\log})$ is a learned stable decay and $\Delta_t$, $B_t$, and $C_t$ depend on the input, so each block selects which time steps to retain at linear cost. Blocks alternate between forward and backward scanning (Fig.~\ref{fig:ssm_block}), giving bidirectional context without extra blocks. The state dimension is eight, with dropout 0.4 and stochastic depth.

\subsection{Local--global fusion and classification}
\label{subsec:tcn}

The state-space output is reduced to 16 channels and concatenated with the 48-channel local sequence $H_c$, so that the head sees both local and global evidence. Two residual grouped temporal convolutional network (TCN) blocks with kernel length four and dilations one and two refine the resulting 64-channel sequence. The average of its last time step and its temporal mean forms the trial representation $q\in\mathbb{R}^{64}$, made of four 16-channel groups (three convolutional scales and the state-space branch). A norm-constrained grouped classifier produces one set of class scores per group, combined with learned softmax weights.

\subsection{Riemannian signal alignment}
\label{subsec:ra}

Because EEG covariance structures are shifted between subjects, Riemannian alignment (RA) whitens each subject's trials by the inverse square root of that subject's mean covariance $\bar{R}$, the affine-invariant Riemannian mean of the OAS-shrunk trial covariances \cite{zanini2018riemannian,he2020euclidean}:
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{X}=\bar{R}^{-1/2}X.
\end{equation}
To avoid leakage, $\bar{R}$ is estimated only from data available before evaluation and then applied unchanged: from the training sessions for the validation sessions of each source subject, and from the unlabeled earlier sessions $\mathcal{T}_a$ for the evaluation sessions $\mathcal{T}_e$ of the target subject.

\subsection{Automatic domain alignment layer}
\label{subsec:autodial}

Riemannian whitening aligns input statistics but not shifts arising inside the network. Following AutoDIAL \cite{carlucci2017autodial}, the trial representation $q$ is therefore normalized by a layer that learns how strongly target features are standardized with their own statistics: source features use the source moments $(\mu_s,\sigma_s^2)$, and target features use the moments of a mixture of the source and target $(\mu_t,\sigma_t^2)$ distributions,
\begin{align}
    \mu_\alpha &= \alpha\mu_t+(1-\alpha)\mu_s, \\
    \sigma_\alpha^2 &= \alpha\sigma_t^2+(1-\alpha)\sigma_s^2+\alpha(1-\alpha)(\mu_t-\mu_s)^2,
\end{align}
so that
\begin{equation}
    \hat{q}^s = \gamma\odot\frac{q^s-\mu_s}{\sqrt{\sigma_s^2+\epsilon}}+\beta, \qquad
    \hat{q}^t = \gamma\odot\frac{q^t-\mu_\alpha}{\sqrt{\sigma_\alpha^2+\epsilon}}+\beta ,
\end{equation}
with shared affine parameters $\gamma$ and $\beta$. The mixing weight $\alpha\in(0,1)$, initialized to 0.5 and learned separately for each of the four feature groups, interpolates between source statistics ($\alpha\rightarrow0$) and target statistics ($\alpha\rightarrow1$). Moments are computed per mini-batch during training and tracked as running averages (momentum 0.1), which are mixed with the learned $\alpha$ at inference; source validation trials always use source statistics.

\subsection{Training objective}
\label{subsec:uda}

A residual $64\!\rightarrow\!128\!\rightarrow\!64$ aligner precedes the classifier and alignment losses. Besides the source cross-entropy $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}$, Stage~1 uses a gradient-reversal domain loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}$ \cite{ganin2016dann}, MK-MMD losses on trial representations ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mmd}}$) and TCN sequences ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tmmd}}$) \cite{long2015dan}, and the information-maximization loss \cite{liang2020shot}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IM}}
    =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} H(p_j)-H\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_j\right),
\label{eq:im}
\end{equation}
where $p_j$ is the predicted class distribution of the $j$-th of $N$ unlabeled trials and $H(\cdot)$ the entropy. The objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}+0.1\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IM}}^{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}
    +0.5\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mmd}}+0.1\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tmmd}}.
\label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IM}}^{t}$ is computed on target trials from $\mathcal{T}_a$. The alignment losses see the AutoDIAL output with $\alpha$ fixed, so $\alpha$ is learned only through $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IM}}^{t}$.

\subsection{Test-time adaptation}
\label{subsec:imtta}

The checkpoint with the highest source-validation accuracy is adapted to the unlabeled evaluation trials $\mathcal{T}_e$ by information-maximization test-time adaptation (IM-TTA): only the BatchNorm scales and shifts are updated, which limits overfitting to a small target set \cite{wang2021tent}, by minimizing Eq.~\eqref{eq:im} over $\mathcal{T}_e$ for five Adam passes (learning rate $10^{-4}$). The AutoDIAL target moments are then re-estimated in one pass over the same trials, with $\alpha$ and the source moments fixed. The labels of $\mathcal{T}_e$ are used only for scoring.

\subsubsection{BCI Competition IV-2a}
\label{subsec:bcic2a}

Nine participants were recorded in two sessions on different days, each with 288 trials of left-hand, right-hand, both-feet, and tongue imagery, from 22 EEG electrodes at 250~Hz (0.5--100~Hz band-pass, 50-Hz notch; the three EOG channels are not used) \cite{tangermann2012review}. Participant sex and age are not reported.

\subsubsection{BCI Competition IV-2b}
\label{subsec:bcic2b}

Nine right-handed participants, drawn from a study of ten (six male, four female; $24.7\pm3.3$ years) \cite{leeb2007virtual}, performed left- versus right-hand imagery recorded from three bipolar derivations around C3, Cz, and C4 at 250~Hz (the three EOG channels are not used). Sessions 1--3 form the calibration portion and sessions 4--5 the evaluation portion \cite{tangermann2012review}; this chronological split is kept, and the dataset tests whether the method remains useful with only three electrodes.

\subsubsection{Zhou2016}
\label{subsec:zhou2016}

Four healthy participants (one male, three female; 22--28 years; sex reported only at group level) were each recorded in three sessions separated by days to months \cite{zhou2016trialselection}. Each session has two runs of 75 trials (25 per class: left hand, right hand, feet) from 14 electrodes at 250~Hz; each trial has a 5-s imagery cue followed by a 4-s rest.

This gives nine folds for BCI IV-2a and IV-2b and four for Zhou2016, with identical hyperparameters in every fold; the checkpoint with the highest source-validation accuracy is used. For Zhou2016, an all-session target score is reported as an auxiliary diagnostic.


\subsection{Preprocessing and augmentation}
\label{subsec:preprocessing}

EEG channels are resampled to 250~Hz and converted to microvolts. BCI IV-2a uses the full MOABB event window and BCI IV-2b drops its last 0.5~s, both unfiltered; Zhou2016 is band-pass filtered at 8--30~Hz and cropped to 0.5--5.0~s after cue onset.

Z-score statistics are fitted on the source-training partition. For BCI IV-2a and IV-2b, labeled source mini-batches are doubled with the segmentation and reconstruction (S\&R) augmentation \cite{lotte2015signal} used by TCFormer \cite{altaheri2025tcformer}, which recombines eight-segment pieces of same-class trials; Zhou2016 uses no augmentation.

\subsection{Implementation details}
\label{subsec:training}

All experiments run in Kaggle notebooks on one NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU (16~GB). BCI IV-2a and IV-2b use a batch size of 48, at most 125 epochs, and Adam (learning rate $9\times10^{-4}$, $\beta_1=0.5$, weight decay $10^{-3}$); Zhou2016 uses a batch size of 32, 100 epochs, a learning rate of $10^{-3}$, and weight decay $10^{-4}$. A three-epoch linear warm-up is followed by cosine decay. The seed (0), data split, and initialization are shared by all methods and variants, so each comparison differs only in the evaluated component.

\subsection{Comparison methods}
\label{subsec:baselines}

Baselines are grouped by target-data access, each group being compared with the SSM-DAN variant of equal access. Source-only decoders (EEG Conformer \cite{song2023eegconformer}, ATCNet \cite{altaheri2023atcnet}, TCFormer \cite{altaheri2025tcformer}, EEGEncoder \cite{liao2025eegencoder}, SATrans-Net \cite{miao2025satransnet}) are compared with SSM-DAN; UDA methods (CLUDA \cite{ozyurt2023cluda}, RMETNet \cite{zhao2026rmetnet}, MSD-DDA \cite{gong2026msdda}), which also see the unlabeled earlier target sessions, with SSM-DAN (Stage~1 UDA), i.e., without IM-TTA; and test-time adaptation methods (T-TIME \cite{li2024ttime}, BFT \cite{li2026bft}) with SSM-DAN (IM-TTA), i.e., without Stage~1 adaptation. All methods share the split, preprocessing, and checkpoint rule.

\subsection{Evaluation measures and reporting}
\label{subsec:metrics}

Accuracy is reported per target subject, and accuracy and Cohen's $\kappa$ as mean $\pm$ standard deviation across folds. Paired accuracy differences use a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test with exact $p$-values. Efficiency is measured by trainable parameters, mean Stage~1 training time per fold on the T4, and batch-one CPU response time (mean of 500 passes after 100 warm-up passes), excluding IM-TTA, preprocessing, and data transfer.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The benefit of each adaptation component is dataset dependent: it was clear on BCI IV-2a but small and not significant on BCI IV-2b and Zhou2016, where repeated-seed experiments are needed to confirm the differences.

The alternating scans exploit complete offline trials and are non-causal, so online use would require forward-only processing.

Because IM-TTA uses unlabeled evaluation-session signals, the method is transductive and must be compared with baselines of identical target access. Further limitations are the three-channel BCI IV-2b setting, the small Zhou2016 cohort, incomplete matched baselines, and the lack of sex-stratified analysis, as participant sex is reported only in aggregate or not at all.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented SSM-DAN, a cross-subject MI-EEG framework that combines multi-scale convolution and bidirectional selective state-space modeling with hierarchical UDA and constrained IM-TTA, evaluated under a chronological LOSO protocol that keeps target labels out of training, adaptation, and model selection. It reached mean accuracies of 76.12\% on BCI IV-2a, 84.09\% on BCI IV-2b, and 84.33\% on Zhou2016. Matched baselines and repeated-seed experiments are still needed before drawing broader conclusions.
