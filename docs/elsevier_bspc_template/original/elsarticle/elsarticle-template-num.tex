\begin{abstract}
Cross-subject motor-imagery electroencephalography (MI-EEG) decoding is hindered by low signal-to-noise ratios, scarce labeled data, and strong distribution shifts between subjects and sessions. To address these challenges, this study proposes SSM-DAN, which builds on a multi-scale convolutional front-end and adds bidirectional selective state-space blocks to model long-range temporal dependencies at a cost that grows linearly with trial length. Subject shift is then reduced step by step: Riemannian alignment first re-centers the EEG covariance of each subject, a learned source--target normalization and distribution matching then align the features, and a test-time adaptation that updates only batch-normalization parameters finally adjusts the decisions on the unlabeled target session. The framework was evaluated on four public MI-EEG datasets using leave-one-subject-out validation with chronologically separated training, adaptation, and test sessions. With a session-specific Riemannian reference, SSM-DAN achieved mean accuracies of 77.74\%, 84.10\%, and 85.33\% on BCI Competition IV-2a, BCI Competition IV-2b, and Zhou2016, the highest of all compared methods. On the 54-subject OpenBMI dataset, it reached a mean accuracy of 84.00\% ($\kappa=0.680$). Ablation studies showed that alignment at the signal, feature, and decision levels plays complementary roles, with relative contributions that vary across datasets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Motor-imagery brain--computer interfaces (MI-BCIs) decode movement intentions from non-invasive electroencephalography (EEG) for rehabilitation, communication, and control. The event-related desynchronization and synchronization patterns on which they rely are weak, non-stationary, and differ between subjects and between sessions of the same subject \cite{pfurtscheller1999erd}. A decoder trained on existing users therefore often degrades on a new one, while calibrating every new user with labeled trials is time-consuming and tiring \cite{lotte2015signal}. Reducing or removing this labeled calibration is thus a central requirement for practical MI-BCIs.

Early MI decoders followed a classical machine-learning pipeline. Common spatial pattern (CSP) filtering \cite{ramoser2000optimal} and its filter-bank extension FBCSP \cite{ang2012filterbank}, which won BCI Competition~IV, extract band-power features through hand-designed, subject-specific stages and pass them to a linear classifier. Deep learning later replaced these stages with end-to-end feature learning: ShallowConvNet and DeepConvNet \cite{schirrmeister2017deep} and the compact EEGNet \cite{lawhern2018eegnet} learn spectral and spatial filters directly from raw EEG. Later models extended the temporal context: EEG Conformer adds self-attention to a convolutional front-end \cite{song2023eegconformer}, ATCNet combines attention with dilated temporal convolutional networks (TCNs) \cite{altaheri2023atcnet}, and TCFormer joins multi-scale convolution, a Transformer, and a TCN \cite{altaheri2025tcformer}. Self-attention, however, has quadratic cost in sequence length \cite{vaswani2017attention} and needs more data than EEG datasets usually provide. More recently, state-space models (SSMs), from the structured S4 \cite{gu2022s4} to the selective Mamba \cite{gu2024mamba}, have emerged as an alternative that scales linearly with sequence length; in language modeling, Mamba matched or exceeded Transformers of comparable size while providing several times higher generation throughput \cite{gu2024mamba}. MI-Mamba and a spatial--temporal Mamba network have since combined SSMs with convolution for MI decoding \cite{guo2025mimamba,yang2025stmamba}. Despite their architectural diversity, these approaches are trained on source data and applied unchanged to new users, so their performance depends on how well the training set represents each new user.

A complementary line of work reduces this dependence on the training population and on per-user labeled calibration. EEG foundation models, pretrained on large and diverse corpora such as the roughly 2,500 hours of EEG used by LaBraM \cite{jiang2024labram,wang2024eegpt}, are a promising direction, but they require collecting and harmonizing large heterogeneous datasets and are usually fine-tuned with labeled data of the target task. Transfer across subjects of the same task is a lighter alternative. Domain generalization learns, from several source subjects, features intended to remain valid for unseen subjects, so that a new user can be served without any calibration \cite{liu2023stdg,zhong2025eegdg,zheng2025crosssubject}; because the target subject is never observed, however, its specific shift cannot be compensated. Domain adaptation removes this limitation by also using unlabeled EEG of the new subject. Such data require no trial labels, which makes domain adaptation a practical route to tailoring the decoder to each user without a labeled calibration session. To align source and target features, the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD), introduced into deep networks in its multi-kernel form by DAN \cite{long2015dan}, is among the most widely used criteria owing to its non-parametric form and stable optimization, and is also adopted by EEG models such as RMETNet \cite{zhao2026rmetnet}. Adversarial training with a domain discriminator \cite{ganin2016dann,tang2020cdan} and prototype-based alignment \cite{zhang2024prototypeda} provide complementary objectives. Alignment can also be performed inside the network through its normalization layers: in visual domain adaptation, AutoDIAL \cite{carlucci2017autodial} introduced domain alignment layers that learn how strongly source and target statistics are mixed during normalization, an idea that motivates the feature-level normalization used in the present work.

Beyond the feature level, subject differences are already present in the raw signals: the covariance between EEG channels, which reflects head anatomy and electrode placement, differs markedly across individuals. This suggests aligning the signals themselves before they enter the network. Treating trial covariances as points on the manifold of symmetric positive-definite matrices, Zanini et al. proposed what is now called Riemannian alignment (RA), which re-centers the covariances of every session at the identity using the Riemannian mean of its resting-state trials \cite{zanini2018riemannian}. The resulting accuracy gain, however, varies across datasets. Moreover, if the reference is computed from a single session, it may no longer fit when the same subject's signals change in later sessions.

Even a decoder adapted to the earlier recordings of a new user meets new data at test time, because a later session differs from the earlier ones. Test-time adaptation addresses this last step by adjusting a trained model on the unlabeled test data themselves before predicting them. SHOT \cite{liang2020shot} adapts a source model with an information-maximization loss, and Tent \cite{wang2021tent} updates only the parameters of the normalization layers. For EEG, T-TIME \cite{li2024ttime} applies test-time information maximization to plug-and-play BCIs, and BFT \cite{li2026bft} adapts lightweight decoders at test time without backpropagation. Signal alignment, feature adaptation, and test-time adaptation thus act at different levels of the decoder.

We therefore propose SSM-DAN, a selective state-space network with hierarchical, label-free adaptation for cross-subject MI decoding, designed to model the temporal dynamics of MI-EEG efficiently while adapting to each new user without labeled calibration. The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a decoder that captures both local spectral--spatial patterns and long-range temporal dependencies of MI-EEG at a cost that grows linearly with trial length;
    \item a hierarchical adaptation strategy that reduces subject shift at the signal, feature, and decision levels using only unlabeled EEG of the new user; and
    \item a chronological leave-one-subject-out evaluation on four public datasets, in which target labels are excluded from training, adaptation, and model selection, and all competing methods are re-trained under identical conditions on BCI IV-2a, BCI IV-2b, and Zhou2016.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\begin{figure*}[p]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.80\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/method_overview_ab.pdf}
    \caption{Architecture of SSM-DAN: (a) SSM backbone and (b) adaptation head. Sizes are channels $\times$ time; $\|$: concatenation; $\oplus$: addition; $\times N$: stacked SSM blocks with alternating scan direction.}
    \label{fig:architecture_overview}
\end{figure*}

The design of SSM-DAN follows the two difficulties raised in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. The first concerns the decoder itself: it must relate information across the whole trial without a cost that grows quickly with trial length. SSM-DAN therefore starts from TCFormer \cite{altaheri2025tcformer}, keeps its convolutional front-end, and replaces its Transformer with selective state-space blocks (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_overview}a; Sections~\ref{subsec:conv_frontend}--\ref{subsec:tcn}). The second concerns the new user: however the decoder is built, it learns only from source subjects, and a new subject can differ from them in the recorded signals, in the features the network extracts, and in the resulting predictions. Rather than correcting this shift at a single point, SSM-DAN reduces it step by step, from the signals to the features and then to the decisions (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_overview}b; Sections~\ref{subsec:ra}--\ref{subsec:imtta}). Because the unlabeled data of the target subject arrive over time, this adaptation takes place in two stages (Fig.~\ref{fig:two_stage}): Stage~1 trains the model on labeled source trials and on the earlier sessions of the target subject, $\mathcal{T}_a$ (adaptation sessions), without their labels; Stage~2 adapts it to the unlabeled evaluation sessions $\mathcal{T}_e$ before predicting them.

\subsection{Multi-scale convolutional encoder}
\label{subsec:conv_frontend}

The front-end keeps the multi-scale convolutional design of TCFormer \cite{altaheri2025tcformer}, in which temporal kernels of different lengths extract local spectral--spatial features at several time scales. Each trial $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times T}$ ($C$ electrodes, $T$ samples) passes through three temporal convolutions with kernels of 20, 32, and 64 samples, followed by depthwise spatial filtering, pooling, and grouped refinement to 16 channels per scale. A residual channel-group attention assigns one sigmoid weight to each scale, and a second pooling yields the local feature sequence $H_c\in\mathbb{R}^{48\times L}$.

\subsection{Bidirectional selective state-space encoder}
\label{subsec:ssm}

Because each convolution covers only a limited receptive field, dependencies that span the trial are usually modeled by a Transformer encoder placed after the convolutional front-end \cite{song2023eegconformer,altaheri2025tcformer}. Self-attention, however, compares every pair of time steps, and its cost therefore grows quadratically with sequence length \cite{vaswani2017attention}. SSM-DAN replaces this encoder with a stack of selective state-space blocks \cite{gu2022s4,gu2024mamba}, which compress the sequence history into a fixed-size latent state through a linear recurrence and therefore run in linear time. Their input-dependent parameterization acts as a content-aware gate that decides, at each time step, whether to retain the state or incorporate the current input \cite{gu2024mamba}, thereby emphasizing task-relevant time steps. Within each block, the multi-head self-attention and feed-forward sublayers are replaced by a linear projection, a depthwise convolution with kernel three, the selective scan, a SiLU gate, and an output projection, preceded by layer normalization and wrapped in a residual connection (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_overview}a). The selective scan drives a diagonal recurrence with the projected input $u_t$:
\begin{align}
    h_t &= \exp(\Delta_t A)\odot h_{t-1} + \Delta_t B_t\odot u_t, \\
    z_t &= C_t^\top h_t + d\odot u_t ,
\end{align}
where $A=-\exp(A_{\log})$ is a learned stable decay and $\Delta_t$, $B_t$, and $C_t$ depend on the input, so each block selects which time steps to retain at linear cost. Blocks alternate between forward and backward scanning (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_overview}a), giving bidirectional context without extra blocks. The state dimension is eight, with dropout 0.4 and stochastic depth.

\subsection{Local--global fusion and classification}
\label{subsec:tcn}

To keep the local evidence of the front-end alongside this global context, the state-space output is reduced to 16 channels and concatenated with the 48-channel local sequence $H_c$. The resulting 64-channel sequence consists of four 16-channel groups, one for each convolutional scale and one for the state-space branch, and is refined by a temporal convolutional network (TCN) \cite{altaheri2023atcnet}. A TCN applies causal convolutions, in which each output depends only on the current and earlier time steps, and increases the dilation from one block to the next so that later blocks cover a wider time window. SSM-DAN uses two residual TCN blocks with kernel length four and dilations one and two; each block contains two causal convolutions, grouped so that the four feature groups are processed separately. The trial representation $q\in\mathbb{R}^{64}$ is the average of the last time step of the TCN output, which summarizes the preceding steps under causal convolution, and its temporal mean. A norm-constrained grouped classifier produces one set of class scores per group, combined with learned softmax weights.

\subsection{Riemannian signal alignment}
\label{subsec:ra}

The encoder above is trained only on source subjects and receives the signals of a new subject as they are. Because the covariance between channels, which depends on head anatomy and electrode placement, differs across subjects (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}), the first adaptation step aligns the signals before they enter the network. Riemannian alignment (RA) is used for this step because it needs no labels, has no trainable parameters, and computes its reference from the subject's own unlabeled trials. RA whitens each subject's trials by the inverse square root of a reference covariance $\bar{R}$, the affine-invariant Riemannian mean of the trial covariances, each estimated with oracle approximating shrinkage (OAS) \cite{chen2010oas}:
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{X}=\bar{R}^{-1/2}X.
\end{equation}
Shrinkage keeps every trial covariance well conditioned, as required by the matrix logarithm of the Riemannian mean and the inverse square root of the reference; this matters most when the channels are linearly dependent, as after the common average reference of OpenBMI, which makes the sample covariance singular. OAS sets the shrinkage intensity from the data, so no parameter is tuned. This combines two existing choices. The reference is the Riemannian mean of Zanini et al. \cite{zanini2018riemannian}, computed here from the unlabeled task trials so that no resting-state recording is needed, and, as in Euclidean alignment \cite{he2020euclidean}, the whitening is applied to the raw trials rather than to their covariances, so that the network can take them as input. The original method re-centers every session, the test session included, with its own reference estimated online. Here, to keep the evaluation session out of every estimate, $\bar{R}$ is estimated only from data available before evaluation and then applied unchanged: from the training sessions for the validation sessions of each source subject, and from $\mathcal{T}_a$ for $\mathcal{T}_e$ of the target subject. A session-specific variant closer to the original, in which every session is whitened by the mean of its own unlabeled trials, is examined in Section~\ref{subsec:ra_reference}.

\subsection{Automatic domain alignment layer}
\label{subsec:autodial}

Once the signals are aligned, the next level is the features that the network extracts from them. Here SSM-DAN borrows the domain alignment layer of AutoDIAL \cite{carlucci2017autodial}, proposed for visual domain adaptation, in which normalization itself learns how far target features should be standardized with their own statistics. Standard batch normalization fixes this choice in advance: target features are standardized either with source statistics, which keeps any difference in mean and variance between the domains, or with their own statistics, which removes it entirely. Because the appropriate degree of alignment is not known beforehand and may differ between feature groups, SSM-DAN lets the network learn it. The layer adds only one parameter per feature group and needs no target labels, so it is trained jointly with the rest of the model on unlabeled target trials. Source features of the trial representation $q$ use the source moments $(\mu_s,\sigma_s^2)$, and target features use the moments of a mixture of the source and target $(\mu_t,\sigma_t^2)$ distributions,
\begin{align}
    \mu_\alpha &= \alpha\mu_t+(1-\alpha)\mu_s, \\
    \sigma_\alpha^2 &= \alpha\sigma_t^2+(1-\alpha)\sigma_s^2+\alpha(1-\alpha)(\mu_t-\mu_s)^2,
\end{align}
so that
\begin{equation}
    \hat{q}^s = \gamma\odot\frac{q^s-\mu_s}{\sqrt{\sigma_s^2+\epsilon}}+\beta, \qquad
    \hat{q}^t = \gamma\odot\frac{q^t-\mu_\alpha}{\sqrt{\sigma_\alpha^2+\epsilon}}+\beta ,
\end{equation}
with shared affine parameters $\gamma$ and $\beta$. The mixing weight $\alpha\in(0,1)$, initialized to 0.5 and learned for each of the four feature groups, moves the target normalization from source statistics ($\alpha\rightarrow0$) to target statistics ($\alpha\rightarrow1$). Moments are computed per mini-batch during training and tracked as running averages (momentum 0.1) for inference; source validation trials use source statistics. After test-time adaptation (Section~\ref{subsec:imtta}), the target moments are re-estimated on the evaluation session with $\alpha$ and the source moments fixed.

\subsection{Training objective}
\label{subsec:uda}

Matching means and variances still leaves other differences between the source and target feature distributions. To reduce them, a residual $64\!\rightarrow\!128\!\rightarrow\!64$ aligner follows the AutoDIAL layer and feeds the classifier and the distribution-level alignment losses. Besides the source cross-entropy $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}$, Stage~1 uses a gradient-reversal domain loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}$ \cite{ganin2016dann}, MK-MMD losses on trial representations ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mmd}}$) and TCN sequences ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tmmd}}$) \cite{long2015dan}, and the information-maximization loss \cite{liang2020shot}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IM}}
    =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} H(p_j)-H\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_j\right),
\label{eq:im}
\end{equation}
where $p_j$ is the predicted class distribution of the $j$-th of $N$ unlabeled trials and $H(\cdot)$ the entropy. The first term makes each prediction confident, and the second keeps the average prediction spread over the classes, which prevents assigning every trial to one class. The objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}+0.1\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IM}}^{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}
    +0.5\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mmd}}+0.1\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tmmd}}.
\label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IM}}^{t}$ is computed on target trials from $\mathcal{T}_a$. The alignment losses see the AutoDIAL output with $\alpha$ fixed, so $\alpha$ is learned only through $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IM}}^{t}$.

\subsection{Test-time adaptation}
\label{subsec:imtta}

After Stage~1, the model is fitted to the target subject, but only to the sessions recorded before evaluation. In practice, a BCI user operates the system over several sessions held at different times, and EEG is non-stationary across sessions \cite{pfurtscheller1999erd}, so the session to be decoded differs from those the model has already seen. Stage~2 (Fig.~\ref{fig:two_stage}) closes this gap by adjusting the model to the unlabeled trials of the evaluation session itself.

This stage combines two test-time adaptation methods: the information-maximization loss of SHOT \cite{liang2020shot}, which makes predictions confident while keeping them balanced across classes, and the choice of Tent \cite{wang2021tent} to update only the parameters of the normalization layers. The resulting information-maximization test-time adaptation (IM-TTA) starts from the selected model and minimizes Eq.~\eqref{eq:im} over the whole evaluation session for five Adam steps, updating only the BatchNorm scales and shifts of the backbone (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}). The diversity term of the loss prevents all trials from being assigned to one class, and updating few parameters keeps the adapted model close to the one it starts from.

\subsubsection{BCI Competition IV-2a}
\label{subsec:bcic2a}

Nine participants were recorded in two sessions on different days, each with 288 trials of left-hand, right-hand, both-feet, and tongue imagery, from 22 EEG electrodes at 250~Hz (0.5--100~Hz band-pass, 50-Hz notch; the three EOG channels are not used) \cite{tangermann2012review}.

\subsubsection{BCI Competition IV-2b}
\label{subsec:bcic2b}

Nine right-handed participants, drawn from a study of ten ($24.7\pm3.3$ years) \cite{leeb2007virtual}, performed left- versus right-hand imagery recorded from three bipolar derivations around C3, Cz, and C4 at 250~Hz (the three EOG channels are not used). Sessions 1--3 form the calibration portion and sessions 4--5 the evaluation portion \cite{tangermann2012review}; this chronological split is kept, and the dataset tests whether the method remains useful with only three electrodes.

\subsubsection{Zhou2016}
\label{subsec:zhou2016}

Four healthy participants (22--28 years) were each recorded in three sessions separated by days to months \cite{zhou2016trialselection}. Each session has two runs of 75 trials (25 per class: left hand, right hand, feet) from 14 electrodes at 250~Hz; each trial has a 5-s imagery cue followed by a 4-s rest.

\subsection{Preprocessing and augmentation}
\label{subsec:preprocessing}

EEG channels are resampled to 250~Hz and converted to microvolts. BCI IV-2a uses the full MOABB event window and BCI IV-2b drops its last 0.5~s, both unfiltered; Zhou2016 is band-pass filtered at 8--30~Hz and cropped to 0.5--5.0~s after cue onset.

Z-score statistics are fitted on the source-training partition. For BCI IV-2a and IV-2b, labeled source mini-batches are doubled with the segmentation and reconstruction (S\&R) augmentation \cite{lotte2015signal} used by TCFormer \cite{altaheri2025tcformer}, which recombines eight-segment pieces of same-class trials; Zhou2016 uses no augmentation.

\subsection{Comparison methods}
\label{subsec:baselines}

Baselines are grouped by target-data access and compared with the SSM-DAN variant of the same access: source-only (EEG Conformer \cite{song2023eegconformer}, ATCNet \cite{altaheri2023atcnet}, TCFormer \cite{altaheri2025tcformer}, EEGEncoder \cite{liao2025eegencoder}, SATrans-Net \cite{miao2025satransnet}), UDA (RMETNet \cite{zhao2026rmetnet}), and test-time adaptation (T-TIME \cite{li2024ttime}, BFT \cite{li2026bft}). All are re-trained from their released code with their recommended optimizer settings, under the same folds, preprocessing, seed, and source-validation checkpoint selection. On Zhou2016, a ridge of $10^{-3}$ times the mean eigenvalue keeps the RMETNet covariances invertible. Published results under other protocols are listed in Section~\ref{subsec:published}.

\subsection{Evaluation measures and reporting}
\label{subsec:metrics}

Accuracy and Cohen's $\kappa$ are reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation over target subjects, together with per-class recall, macro-F1, and the information transfer rate (ITR) \cite{wolpaw2002bci}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ITR}=\frac{60}{T}\left[\log_2 N+P\log_2 P+(1-P)\log_2\frac{1-P}{N-1}\right]\ \text{bits/min},
\label{eq:itr}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the accuracy, $N$ the number of classes, and $T$ the decoded window (4~s for BCI IV-2a/2b, 4.5~s for Zhou2016); ITR at or below chance is set to zero. Paired differences are tested with exact two-sided Wilcoxon tests, Holm-corrected within each target-access group ($p_\mathrm{Holm}<0.05$). Efficiency is measured by parameter count, Stage~1 training time, and single-trial CPU latency.

\subsection{Information transfer rate and class-wise performance}
\label{subsec:itr_classwise}

In practical terms, the accuracy gains of SSM-DAN translate into faster communication. On BCI IV-2a, adaptation raised its ITR by about 76\% over the source-only variant ($13.3$ versus $7.5$~bits/min; Table~\ref{tab:results_2a}), so a new user could issue markedly more commands per minute without any labeled calibration. On BCI IV-2b and Zhou2016 the adaptive methods operated in a similar range, mirroring the accuracy results.

\subsection{Comparison with published results}
\label{subsec:published}

Table~\ref{tab:published} sets the accuracies reported in the original articles beside our re-runs. The two are not directly comparable, because most published protocols are easier or more optimistic than ours in at least one of four respects: (i) the T-TIME and BFT articles report two-class left- versus right-hand MI, whereas BCI IV-2a and Zhou2016 are evaluated here with four and three classes; (ii) several protocols train and test within one session, and the EEGEncoder figure includes the test subjects' first sessions in training, whereas every target here is unseen and scored on a later day; (iii) the released SATrans-Net and RMETNet scripts select the epoch with the evaluation labels, whereas we select on source data only; and (iv) RMETNet adapts to the evaluation trials themselves, whereas our Stage~1 sees only earlier, unlabeled sessions. These differences largely explain why most re-runs are lower on BCI IV-2a. Where the split matches ours, the gaps are small: the TCFormer benchmark \cite{altaheri2025tcformer} uses the same session split, differing mainly in five-seed averaging, and our re-runs of TCFormer and ATCNet lie within three points of it on both BCI datasets, while EEG Conformer is higher here. On BCI IV-2b, whose sessions shift less, the re-runs reach or exceed several published values (e.g., SATrans-Net, 81.76 vs.\ 79.64\%).

\subsection{Ablation study}
\label{sec:ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} removes one component at a time under the chronological protocol. The variant without domain adaptation drops all Stage~1 alignment (AutoDIAL, target information maximization, and the adversarial and both MMD losses) but keeps RA and IM-TTA. To test the components on a larger cohort, the ablation adds OpenBMI \cite{lee2019openbmi}: 54 subjects, 62 channels, and two sessions on different days, each with 100 left- versus right-hand MI trials. Signals are band-pass filtered at 4--38~Hz, common-average referenced, downsampled to 100~Hz, and cut to 0--4~s after the cue, without S\&R. Session~1 provides the source training data and the unlabeled target data of Stage~1, session~2 the source validation and target evaluation data; RA is fitted on session~1, and the BCI IV-2a optimization settings are used. Since every evaluation session has 100 balanced trials, $\kappa=2P-1$; the OpenBMI Wilcoxon tests use the normal approximation, with Holm correction over the four ablation comparisons.

On BCI IV-2a, removing RA, Stage~1 domain adaptation, or IM-TTA each significantly reduced accuracy; on BCI IV-2b and Zhou2016, the complete model remained the best variant, but by small, non-significant margins. OpenBMI behaved differently: IM-TTA added 3.96 points (38 of 54 targets, $p_\mathrm{Holm}<0.001$), the gains over the variants without domain adaptation (0.76 points) and without any adaptation (0.51 points) were not significant ($p_\mathrm{Holm}=0.68$ and $0.72$), and removing RA raised accuracy by 2.89 points (33 of 54, $p_\mathrm{Holm}=0.002$).

Two follow-up comparisons, made after the main experiments and not Holm-corrected, examine these effects. In the first, the variant without both RA and domain adaptation was run in a later batch together with a rerun of the variant without RA, which reached 66.63, 83.10, 81.17, and 81.54\% on BCI IV-2a, BCI IV-2b, Zhou2016, and OpenBMI. Within this batch, removing domain adaptation lowered accuracy by 4.59 points on BCI IV-2a (7 of 9 targets, exact $p=0.36$), 3.09 on BCI IV-2b (6 of 9, $p=0.098$), 8.33 on Zhou2016 (3 of 4, one tie, $p=0.25$), and 1.31 on OpenBMI (33 of 54, $p=0.038$). In the second, the RA reference was estimated for each session from its own unlabeled trials, the same trials that IM-TTA uses. Trial energy changed more between sessions on OpenBMI than on BCI IV-2a: after session-1 RA, the median trial energy of session~2 was 1.55 times that of session~1 on OpenBMI, and more than twice for 20 of the 54 subjects, against 1.08--1.39 on BCI IV-2a. The session-specific reference raised accuracy by 3.39 points on OpenBMI (43 of 54, $p<0.001$) and 1.62 points on BCI IV-2a (8 of 9, exact $p=0.023$), left BCI IV-2b unchanged, and added 1.00 point on Zhou2016 (3 of 4, $p=0.38$) (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). With this reference, IM-TTA still added 2.89 points on BCI IV-2a ($p=0.008$) and 2.83 on Zhou2016 ($p=0.125$), but only 0.11 on OpenBMI ($p=0.50$).

\subsection{Computational efficiency}
\label{subsec:results_efficiency}

The cost columns of Tables~\ref{tab:results_2a}--\ref{tab:results_zhou} report trainable parameters, Stage~1 training time, and CPU response time. The source-only variant is smaller because it omits the AutoDIAL layer, aligner, and domain discriminator; RA and IM-TTA do not change parameter count.

Stage~1 adaptation accounts for most of the extra training time of SSM-DAN over its source-only variant (nearly doubling it on Zhou2016), whereas IM-TTA adds little. SSM-DAN has a parameter count comparable to ATCNet and TCFormer and far below EEG Conformer, while T-TIME and BFT are the smallest and fastest methods.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

With a session-specific RA reference, SSM-DAN achieved the highest mean accuracy among the compared methods on BCI IV-2a, BCI IV-2b, and Zhou2016. Without adaptation, the state-space backbone was already competitive with the attention-based decoders while using the fewest parameters among the source-only models (Tables~\ref{tab:results_2a}--\ref{tab:results_zhou}). The larger margins appeared once adaptation was added, so the two parts play complementary roles: the backbone provides a compact temporal representation, and the hierarchical adaptation carries it across subjects.

At the signal level, the value of RA depended on the dataset: it helped most on BCI IV-2a, mattered little on BCI IV-2b and Zhou2016, and lowered accuracy on OpenBMI when its reference was fixed on the first session (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The OpenBMI result points to the reference rather than to the alignment itself\label{subsec:ra_reference}: signal energy changed markedly between the two sessions of many OpenBMI subjects, more than on BCI IV-2a, so a reference estimated on one session no longer whitened the next. Estimating the reference from each session's own unlabeled trials removed this mismatch, with the largest gain on OpenBMI, a smaller one on BCI IV-2a, and little change on BCI IV-2b and Zhou2016 (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). This supports the per-session re-centering of the original RA \cite{zanini2018riemannian}: when a user's signals drift between sessions, the reference is better re-estimated for each session than kept fixed.

At the feature level, removing Stage~1 domain adaptation alone mattered mainly on BCI IV-2a, but its role became clearer when RA was removed as well. On BCI IV-2b and Zhou2016, either level alone could largely stand in for the other, whereas removing both caused a much larger drop; on BCI IV-2a, the losses of the two levels added up (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). Feature-level alignment also improved accuracy without RA on every dataset (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), so it is useful on its own rather than only as a complement to RA. Because the variant without both levels was run in a later batch, the comparison on BCI IV-2b, which lies within the variation between reruns, is only indicative.

At the decision level, the benefit of IM-TTA depended on the RA reference. With the fixed reference, it improved accuracy most clearly on BCI IV-2a and OpenBMI; with the session-specific reference, its gain on OpenBMI almost vanished, whereas it remained on BCI IV-2a and Zhou2016 (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). On OpenBMI, IM-TTA thus mainly compensated for the change in signal energy between sessions, which the session-specific reference already removes, while on the other datasets it also corrects shifts that RA leaves.

Among the baselines, T-TIME, which also adapts to unlabeled target trials at test time, was the strongest on BCI IV-2a and Zhou2016, in line with the value of target-data access seen in the ablations. Because IM-TTA and the session-specific reference use unlabeled evaluation-session signals, SSM-DAN is transductive, and its fairest comparison is with methods of the same target access. All results come from a single seed, Zhou2016 has few subjects and BCI IV-2b few channels, and no baselines were run on OpenBMI, so small differences, such as the margin over T-TIME on Zhou2016, should not be overinterpreted. The alternating scans exploit complete offline trials and are non-causal, and both IM-TTA and the session-specific reference need the evaluation trials as a batch. Online use would therefore require forward-only scanning and running estimates of the RA reference and of the adapted statistics, which remain to be developed and evaluated together with repeated-seed experiments.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented SSM-DAN, a cross-subject MI-EEG framework that combines multi-scale convolution and bidirectional selective state-space modeling with hierarchical UDA and constrained IM-TTA, evaluated under a chronological LOSO protocol that keeps target labels out of training, adaptation, and model selection. With a session-specific RA reference, it reached mean accuracies of 77.74\% on BCI IV-2a, 84.10\% on BCI IV-2b, 85.33\% on Zhou2016, and 84.00\% on the 54-subject OpenBMI dataset, the highest of all compared methods on the first three datasets and of all ablation variants on OpenBMI. Repeated-seed experiments and baselines on OpenBMI are still needed before drawing broader conclusions.
