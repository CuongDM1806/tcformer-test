\begin{abstract}
Cross-subject motor-imagery electroencephalography (MI-EEG) decoding is hindered by low signal-to-noise ratios, scarce labeled data, and strong distribution shifts between subjects and sessions. This study proposes SSM-DAN, a domain-adaptive selective state-space network that couples a multi-scale convolutional front-end with bidirectional selective state-space blocks to capture both local and long-range temporal patterns, and reduces subject shift through hierarchical alignment, including a learned source--target normalization layer and label-free test-time adaptation. The framework was evaluated on three public MI-EEG datasets using leave-one-subject-out validation with chronologically separated training, adaptation, and test sessions. On BCI Competition IV-2a, it reached a mean accuracy of 76.12\%, significantly higher than its source-only variant (64.35\%; $p=0.0039$) and its variant without test-time adaptation (72.15\%; $p=0.0039$). It also achieved 84.09\% on BCI Competition IV-2b and 84.33\% on Zhou2016, where it outperformed a matched TCFormer baseline on all four target subjects. Ablation studies showed that the contribution of each adaptation component depends on the dataset. These results indicate that combining selective state-space modeling with learned, hierarchical alignment improves cross-subject MI decoding without target labels.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Motor-imagery brain--computer interfaces (MI-BCIs) decode movement intentions from non-invasive electroencephalography (EEG) for rehabilitation, assistive communication, and human--machine control. Their principal signatures, event-related desynchronization and synchronization, are weak, non-stationary, and distributed across time and electrodes \cite{pfurtscheller1999erd}, and they vary substantially between subjects and recording sessions. A model trained on existing users therefore often degrades on a new user, while collecting labeled calibration trials for every user is time-consuming. Cross-subject decoding must learn discriminative spatial--temporal structure from limited EEG and control inter-subject shift without target labels.

Classical pipelines such as common spatial pattern (CSP) and filter-bank CSP (FBCSP) rely on manually designed stages and subject-specific covariance structure that transfers poorly across users \cite{ramoser2000optimal,ang2012filterbank}. End-to-end ConvNets and EEGNet learn temporal and electrode filters directly from EEG \cite{schirrmeister2017deep,lawhern2018eegnet} but relate distant time points only through depth, large kernels, or dilation. Hybrid decoders such as EEG Conformer, ATCNet, and TCFormer add self-attention and dilated temporal convolutional networks (TCNs) for global context \cite{song2023eegconformer,altaheri2023atcnet,altaheri2025tcformer}, at a quadratic sequence cost \cite{vaswani2017attention} and with a high data demand in small BCI datasets. Selective state-space models provide linear-complexity sequence modeling \cite{gu2022s4,gu2024mamba}, and recent MI decoders combine convolution with Mamba \cite{guo2025mimamba,yang2025stmamba}; however, these decoders mainly study the architecture, use forward-only scans although complete offline trials are available, and do not address subject shift.

Domain generalization learns subject-invariant features from source subjects only \cite{liu2023stdg,zhong2025eegdg,zheng2025crosssubject}, whereas unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) also exploits unlabeled target EEG through Riemannian or Euclidean alignment \cite{zanini2018riemannian,he2020euclidean}, MK-MMD \cite{long2015dan}, or adversarial learning with gradient reversal \cite{ganin2016dann,tang2020cdan}. HADANet combines the latter two for MI-EEG \cite{wu2026hadanet}, prototype-based methods exploit class structure \cite{zhang2024prototypeda}, and AutoDIAL aligns domains inside normalization layers by learning how to mix source and target statistics \cite{carlucci2017autodial}. Test-time adaptation further reduces residual shift at inference through information maximization \cite{liang2020shot} or normalization-only updates \cite{wang2021tent}. Alignment with a fixed strength, however, risks negative transfer when the shift is small, alignment of pooled trial features alone can overlook temporal mismatch, and test-time adaptation accesses evaluation-session signals, which must be disclosed and kept separate from labels.

To our knowledge, selective state-space MI decoders have not been coupled with target adaptation, and existing EEG adaptation methods neither learn how strongly to align nor align temporal representations. We address these gaps with SSM-DAN, a selective state-space model with domain adaptation that couples a bidirectional selective state-space encoder with hierarchical, label-free adaptation, evaluated under a chronological leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) protocol in which target labels remain hidden throughout preprocessing, training, checkpoint selection, and adaptation. The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item an end-to-end decoder that combines multi-scale convolution, alternating forward/backward selective state-space blocks, and local--global TCN fusion;
    \item a hierarchical adaptation strategy that combines Riemannian alignment, an AutoDIAL normalization layer, adversarial and MK-MMD alignment, and constrained test-time adaptation within a chronological, leakage-controlled LOSO protocol; and
    \item an evaluation on three public MI-EEG datasets with subject-wise accuracy, Cohen's $\kappa$, statistical tests, efficiency analysis, and component ablations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

SSM-DAN has two parts (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture_overview}). An EEG encoder combines multi-scale convolutions for local spectral--spatial features, bidirectional selective state-space blocks for long-range dynamics, and a temporal convolutional fusion head (Sections~\ref{subsec:conv_frontend}--\ref{subsec:tcn}). A hierarchical adaptation scheme reduces subject shift at three levels: Riemannian whitening of the input, AutoDIAL normalization with adversarial and discrepancy losses on the features, and test-time adaptation of the predictions (Sections~\ref{subsec:ra}--\ref{subsec:imtta}). The model is trained on labeled source-subject trials and unlabeled target-subject trials (Stage~1), adapted to the unlabeled evaluation trials (Stage~2), and then used for prediction (Stage~3).

\subsection{Multi-scale convolutional encoder}
\label{subsec:conv_frontend}

Each trial is an EEG matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times T}$ with $C$ electrodes and $T$ samples. Following TCFormer \cite{altaheri2025tcformer}, three temporal convolutions with kernels of 20, 32, and 64 samples each produce 32 feature maps at a different time scale (Fig.~\ref{fig:encoder_detail}). A depthwise convolution over all electrodes learns two spatial filters per map, followed by average pooling and dropout. A grouped pointwise convolution reduces the 192 maps to 48 channels (16 per scale), refined by a grouped temporal convolution. A residual channel-group attention reweights each scale with one learned coefficient, and a second pooling stage yields the local feature sequence $H_c\in\mathbb{R}^{48\times L}$ with $L$ pooled time steps.

\begin{figure*}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/encoder_detail.pdf}
    \caption{Multi-scale temporal--spatial front-end. (a) Three temporal branches, depthwise electrode filtering, grouped refinement, and channel-group attention produce $H_c$. (b) Channel-group attention: grouped $1\times1$ squeeze--excitation yields one sigmoid weight per temporal scale, which rescales that scale's 16 channels and is added residually.}
    \label{fig:encoder_detail}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Bidirectional selective state-space encoder}
\label{subsec:ssm}

To capture trial-wide dependencies, $H_c$ is mixed across channels and passed through five selective state-space blocks \cite{gu2022s4,gu2024mamba}. Each block normalizes its input and, through gated projections and a short causal convolution, produces a sequence $u_t$ that drives a diagonal recurrence:
\begin{align}
    h_t &= \exp(\Delta_t A)\odot h_{t-1} + \Delta_t B_t\odot u_t, \\
    z_t &= C_t^\top h_t + d\odot u_t ,
\end{align}
where $h_t$ is the hidden state, $A=-\exp(A_{\log})$ is a learned negative decay that keeps the recurrence stable, and $\Delta_t$, $B_t$, and $C_t$ are computed from the input, so each block selects which time steps to retain at a cost linear in $L$. Since complete trials are available offline, blocks alternate between forward and backward scanning, a backward block reversing the time axis around its recurrence (Fig.~\ref{fig:ssm_block}), which gives bidirectional context without duplicating blocks. The state dimension is eight, with dropout 0.4 and depth-dependent stochastic depth; the recurrence is implemented in plain PyTorch rather than with the Mamba CUDA kernel.

    \caption{Selective SSM block. Backward blocks reverse and restore the temporal axis around the mixer; forward blocks omit these operations.}
    \label{fig:ssm_block}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Local--global fusion and classification}
\label{subsec:tcn}

The state-space output is reduced to 16 channels and concatenated with the 48-channel local sequence $H_c$, so that the head sees both local and global evidence. Two residual grouped temporal convolutional network (TCN) blocks with kernel length four and dilations one and two refine the resulting 64-channel sequence. The average of its last time step and its temporal mean forms the trial representation $q\in\mathbb{R}^{64}$, made of four 16-channel groups (three convolutional scales and the state-space branch). A norm-constrained grouped classifier produces one set of class scores per group, combined with learned softmax weights.

\subsection{Riemannian signal alignment}
\label{subsec:ra}

Because EEG covariance structures are shifted between subjects, Riemannian alignment (RA) whitens each subject's trials by the inverse square root of that subject's mean covariance $\bar{R}$, the affine-invariant Riemannian mean of the OAS-shrunk trial covariances \cite{zanini2018riemannian,he2020euclidean}:
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{X}=\bar{R}^{-1/2}X.
\end{equation}
To avoid leakage, $\bar{R}$ is estimated only from data available before evaluation and then applied unchanged: from the training sessions for the validation sessions of each source subject, and from the unlabeled earlier sessions $\mathcal{T}_a$ for the evaluation sessions $\mathcal{T}_e$ of the target subject.

\subsection{Automatic domain alignment layer}
\label{subsec:autodial}

Riemannian whitening aligns input statistics but not shifts arising inside the network. Following AutoDIAL \cite{carlucci2017autodial}, the trial representation $q$ is therefore normalized by a layer that learns how strongly target features are standardized with their own statistics: source features use the source moments $(\mu_s,\sigma_s^2)$, and target features use the moments of a mixture of the source and target $(\mu_t,\sigma_t^2)$ distributions,
\begin{align}
    \mu_\alpha &= \alpha\mu_t+(1-\alpha)\mu_s, \\
    \sigma_\alpha^2 &= \alpha\sigma_t^2+(1-\alpha)\sigma_s^2+\alpha(1-\alpha)(\mu_t-\mu_s)^2,
\end{align}
so that
\begin{equation}
    \hat{q}^s = \gamma\odot\frac{q^s-\mu_s}{\sqrt{\sigma_s^2+\epsilon}}+\beta, \qquad
    \hat{q}^t = \gamma\odot\frac{q^t-\mu_\alpha}{\sqrt{\sigma_\alpha^2+\epsilon}}+\beta ,
\end{equation}
with shared affine parameters $\gamma$ and $\beta$. The mixing weight $\alpha\in(0,1)$, initialized to 0.5 and learned separately for each of the four feature groups, interpolates between source statistics ($\alpha\rightarrow0$) and target statistics ($\alpha\rightarrow1$). Moments are computed per mini-batch during training and tracked as running averages (momentum 0.1), which are mixed with the learned $\alpha$ at inference; source validation trials always use source statistics.

\subsection{Training objective}
\label{subsec:uda}

A residual $64\!\rightarrow\!128\!\rightarrow\!64$ aligner maps the normalized representation into the space used by the classifier and the alignment losses. Besides the source cross-entropy $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}$, the only labeled term, a gradient-reversal domain discriminator \cite{ganin2016dann} gives $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}$, with a weight ramped from zero to one, and a five-kernel MK-MMD \cite{long2015dan} aligns source and target trial representations ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mmd}}$) and time-averaged TCN sequences ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tmmd}}$). Target predictions are made confident and class-balanced by the information-maximization loss \cite{liang2020shot}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IM}}
    =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} H(p_j)-H\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_j\right),
\label{eq:im}
\end{equation}
where $p_j$ is the predicted class distribution of the $j$-th of $N$ unlabeled trials and $H(\cdot)$ the entropy. With $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IM}}^{t}$ computed on a target mini-batch, the objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}+0.1\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IM}}^{t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}
    +0.5\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mmd}}+0.1\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tmmd}}.
\label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
Target trials in this stage come only from $\mathcal{T}_a$. The alignment losses see the AutoDIAL output with $\alpha$ fixed, since they could otherwise be minimized trivially by $\alpha\rightarrow1$; $\alpha$ is thus learned only through $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IM}}^{t}$.

\subsection{Test-time adaptation}
\label{subsec:imtta}

The checkpoint with the highest source-validation accuracy is adapted to the unlabeled evaluation trials $\mathcal{T}_e$ by information-maximization test-time adaptation (IM-TTA): only the BatchNorm scales and shifts are updated, which limits overfitting to a small target set \cite{wang2021tent}, by minimizing Eq.~\eqref{eq:im} over $\mathcal{T}_e$ for five Adam passes (learning rate $10^{-4}$). The AutoDIAL target moments are then re-estimated in one pass over the same trials, with $\alpha$ and the source moments fixed. The labels of $\mathcal{T}_e$ are used only for scoring.

\subsubsection{BCI Competition IV-2a}
\label{subsec:bcic2a}

Nine participants were recorded in two sessions on different days, each with 288 trials of left-hand, right-hand, both-feet, and tongue imagery, from 22 EEG electrodes at 250~Hz (0.5--100~Hz band-pass, 50-Hz notch; the three EOG channels are not used) \cite{tangermann2012review}. Participant sex and age are not reported.

\subsubsection{BCI Competition IV-2b}
\label{subsec:bcic2b}

Nine right-handed participants, drawn from a study of ten (six male, four female; $24.7\pm3.3$ years) \cite{leeb2007virtual}, performed left- versus right-hand imagery recorded from three bipolar derivations around C3, Cz, and C4 at 250~Hz (the three EOG channels are not used). Sessions 1--3 form the calibration portion and sessions 4--5 the evaluation portion \cite{tangermann2012review}; this chronological split is kept, and the dataset tests whether the method remains useful with only three electrodes.

\subsubsection{Zhou2016}
\label{subsec:zhou2016}

Four healthy participants (one male, three female; 22--28 years; sex reported only at group level) were each recorded in three sessions separated by days to months \cite{zhou2016trialselection}. Each session has two runs of 75 trials (25 per class: left hand, right hand, feet) from 14 electrodes at 250~Hz; each trial has a 5-s imagery cue followed by a 4-s rest.

\subsection{Chronological LOSO protocol with test-time adaptation}
\label{subsec:clean_protocol}

Each LOSO fold holds out one target subject and uses all others as labeled sources, with the chronological session split in Table~\ref{tab:protocol}. Earlier target sessions are used without labels for Stage~1 UDA. The later evaluation sessions are excluded from training, preprocessing-statistic fitting, and checkpoint selection; at inference, their unlabeled EEG is used only by IM-TTA and AutoDIAL target-statistic re-estimation, after which the withheld labels are used for scoring. This is the primary protocol for all comparisons.

This yields nine folds for BCI IV-2a and IV-2b and four for Zhou2016, with identical rules and hyperparameters in every fold. The checkpoint with the highest source-validation accuracy is selected before IM-TTA, so evaluation labels and metrics never influence adaptation or model selection. For Zhou2016, an all-session target score is reported as an auxiliary diagnostic only.


\subsection{Preprocessing and augmentation}
\label{subsec:preprocessing}

Only EEG channels are retained, resampled to 250~Hz, and converted to microvolts. BCI IV-2a uses the full MOABB event window and BCI IV-2b removes its last 0.5~s, both without further filtering. Zhou2016 is band-pass filtered at 8--30~Hz and cropped to 0.5--5.0~s after cue onset (1125 samples) to suppress the cue transient and focus on the $\mu/\beta$ range.

Riemannian whitening follows the session-isolated rule in Section~\ref{subsec:ra}, and z-score statistics are fitted on the source-training partition and then frozen. Following TCFormer \cite{altaheri2025tcformer}, segmentation and reconstruction (S\&R) augmentation \cite{lotte2015signal} is applied to labeled source mini-batches of BCI IV-2a and IV-2b: each trial is split into eight consecutive segments, and synthetic trials concatenate same-position segments from randomly chosen same-class trials, doubling the mini-batch without using target data. Zhou2016 uses no augmentation.

\subsection{Implementation details}
\label{subsec:training}

All experiments run in Kaggle notebooks on one NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU (16~GB). BCI IV-2a and IV-2b use a batch size of 48, at most 125 epochs, and Adam (learning rate $9\times10^{-4}$, $\beta_1=0.5$, weight decay $10^{-3}$); Zhou2016 uses a batch size of 32, 100 epochs, a learning rate of $10^{-3}$, and weight decay $10^{-4}$. A three-epoch linear warm-up is followed by cosine decay. The seed (0), data split, and initialization are shared by all methods and variants, so each comparison differs only in the evaluated component.

\subsection{Comparison methods}
\label{subsec:baselines}

Baselines are grouped by the target data they access, and each group is compared with the SSM-DAN variant that has the same access. Source-only decoders (EEG Conformer \cite{song2023eegconformer}, ATCNet \cite{altaheri2023atcnet}, TCFormer \cite{altaheri2025tcformer}, EEGEncoder \cite{liao2025eegencoder}, and SATrans-Net \cite{miao2025satransnet}) use labeled source data only. Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) methods (CLUDA \cite{ozyurt2023cluda}, RMETNet \cite{zhao2026rmetnet}, and MSD-DDA \cite{gong2026msdda}) also use the unlabeled earlier target sessions, as Stage~1 of SSM-DAN does, without test-time adaptation; their reference is SSM-DAN (Stage~1 UDA), i.e., SSM-DAN without IM-TTA. Test-time adaptation methods (T-TIME \cite{li2024ttime} and BFT \cite{li2026bft}) train on source data only and adapt to the unlabeled evaluation trials; their reference is SSM-DAN (IM-TTA), i.e., SSM-DAN without any Stage~1 adaptation component. Both references keep Riemannian alignment. All methods share the session split, preprocessing, and checkpoint rule.

\subsection{Evaluation measures and reporting}
\label{subsec:metrics}

Accuracy and Cohen's $\kappa$ ($\times100$) are reported per target subject and as mean $\pm$ standard deviation across folds. Paired accuracy differences use a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test with exact $p$-values. Efficiency is measured by trainable parameters, mean Stage~1 training time per fold on the T4, and batch-one CPU response time (mean of 500 passes after 100 warm-up passes), excluding IM-TTA, preprocessing, and data transfer.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results show that adaptation is dataset dependent. Among its variants, SSM-DAN was best on all three datasets, although on BCI IV-2b its margin over the variant without test-time adaptation was only 0.12 percentage points and all variants were statistically indistinguishable. On Zhou2016, SSM-DAN also had the lowest variability across targets, but its margins over the variants without Riemannian alignment, test-time adaptation, or Stage~1 domain adaptation were small and cannot be significant with four folds. Filtering to 8--30~Hz and excluding the first 0.5~s focus covariance estimation on MI-related activity rather than broadband drift and cue transients; repeated-seed experiments are still required to establish the stability of these differences.

Architecturally, multi-scale and depthwise convolutions preserve local spectral--spatial evidence, while alternating selective scans and the TCN model complementary long- and short-range dynamics. The alternating scans exploit complete offline trials and are non-causal; online use would require forward-only processing and device-specific latency validation.

The method is transductive because IM-TTA accesses unlabeled evaluation-session signals before scoring. Comparisons must therefore provide baselines with identical target access. Other limitations include the three-channel BCI IV-2b setting, the small Zhou2016 cohort, and the absence of repeated-seed and fully matched baseline results. Participant sex is reported only in aggregate or not at all for the public datasets, and the cohorts are too small for sex- or gender-stratified analysis; potential sex-related differences in decoding performance were therefore not assessed and limit the generalizability of the findings.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented SSM-DAN, a cross-subject MI-EEG framework that combines multi-scale convolution and bidirectional selective state-space modeling with hierarchical UDA and constrained IM-TTA, evaluated under a chronological LOSO protocol that keeps target labels out of training, adaptation, and model selection. It reached mean accuracies of 76.12\% on BCI IV-2a, 84.09\% on BCI IV-2b, and 84.33\% on Zhou2016. Matched baselines and repeated-seed experiments are still needed before drawing broader conclusions.
